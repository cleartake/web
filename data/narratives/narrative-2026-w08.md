The three-week recovery is over. The aggregate index fell nearly 6% — its worst week since the April 2025 tariff selloff — erasing in five days what took three weeks to rebuild. Only four of fifty companies finished Monday in the green.

Two forces hit at once. A widely circulated research report on AI-driven job displacement triggered a violent selloff in software and platform stocks, while a Supreme Court ruling striking down Trump's reciprocal tariffs — followed by an immediate escalation to 15% global tariffs — added a macro shock on top. The combination produced the kind of broad, indiscriminate selling that overwhelms fundamentals.

The damage was not evenly distributed. Platform fell over 8.5%, its worst week in the index's history. Application dropped 7.5%. Compute, by contrast, declined just 1.6%. On Monday's worst day, IBM fell 13% on AI disruption fears, MongoDB and Datadog each dropped 11%, and most Platform names were deep in the red. Meanwhile, NVIDIA actually finished the day positive.
This is the starkest layer divergence of the year, but in the opposite direction from January. In January, Compute led the gains. Now it's absorbing the least damage. The pattern is consistent: infrastructure holds up better than the layers that depend on it, whether markets are rising or falling.

Year-to-date, the divergence has become extreme. Compute is still up nearly 9%. Platform has fallen to roughly -7.5%. Application is now down over 10%. The aggregate, which was up 3% just a week ago, has swung to -3%.

Bubble readings actually fell — the natural result of prices dropping faster than valuations can adjust. The aggregate eased to 0.38. Application's readings declined across the board, suggesting some of the speculative excess we flagged in recent weeks is being wrung out by price action rather than fundamentals.

Last week we asked whether Platform and Application could sustain momentum on their own. They couldn't. The downstream layers remain vulnerable to sentiment shifts in ways that Compute simply isn't — partly because infrastructure demand is backed by hundreds of billions in committed capex, and partly because AI disruption fears cut against software companies, not the hardware powering them.
